Abstract
Purpose: To develop an orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based automated measurement tool for exophthalmos in thyroid-associated orbitopathy (TAO).
Materials and Methods: One senior radiologist and two junior radiologists (Junior No.1 and Junior No.2) calculated exophthalmos measurements based on 2D and 3D MRI data, respectively, in 177 patients with TAO. Hertel-based exophthalmos measurements were performed in 122 of these patients. Following MRI-based exophthalmos measurement procedures, models for automatically identifying the most anterior point of the zygomatic prominence and orbital segmentation were developed. An automated tool for assessing exophthalmos using orbital MRI was developed. The consistency of exophthalmos measurements across different methods was also evaluated.
Results: The results of the paired t-test indicated a high degree of consistency between 2D and 3D orbital MRI, with no significant differences observed. In the 2D MRI analysis, no statistically significant differences were found between the senior radiologist and Junior No.1. However, both the senior radiologist and Junior No.1 exhibited statistically significant discrepancies when compared with Junior No. 2 (p<0.05) in both 2D and 3D MRI. All intergroup comparisons of 3D MRI revealed significant differences. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation (r = 0.75-0.77) between Hertel exophthalmos measurements and orbital MRI-based assessments. 工具的效果与一致性检验
Conclusions:
Orbital MRI-based exophthalmos demonstrated a strong correlation with Hertel exophthalmometer measurements, although with inherent inter-observer variability. The implementation of an automated orbital MRI assessment tool offers superior accuracy and objectivity in quantifying proptosis.

Introduction
Thyroid-associated orbitopathy (TAO), also known as Graves’ ophthalmopathy, is an organ-specific autoimmune disease closely related to autoimmune thyroid disease(1–3). TAO, the most common orbital disease in adults, causes muscle hypertrophy, eyeball protrusion, diplopia, optic nerve compression, and vision loss, affecting patients' quality of life.
	According to the 2021 European Group on Graves' orbitopathy (EUGOGO) clinical practice guidelines, the clinical activity score and severity grading are important indicators for the evaluation of TAO and determination of further treatment options(2). The change of exophthalmos, however, is included in the Clinical Activity Score (10 items) and related efficacy evaluation details as an important assessment indicator and is usually based on a threshold criterion of 2 mm(3–5). Although Hertel-based measurements are considered to be the gold standard for proptosis measurements, available studies have shown that the accuracy of the measurements is dependent on the ophthalmologist's experience, which is unreliable and subjective(6,7). 
Given the advantages of radiation-free properties and high soft-tissue resolution, orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has become an important part of the routine examination of patients with TAO(5,8,9). It can provide more detailed information of the extraocular muscles and optic nerve, which are critical for assessing the level of TAO activity and predicting treatment outcomes. In addition, orbital MRI can also provide accurate measurements of proptosis. It has been shown that there is a direct correlation between orbital MRI-based proptosis measurements and the conventional Hertel exophthalmometry value(10). 
 However, the use of orbital MRI for proptosis measurements by different radiologists can result in inconsistent outcomes due to subjective judgment and variations in individual skills, which may impact the accuracy of diagnostic and therapeutic decisions. xFurthermore, in clinical practice, some participants' eyes are not directed forward in orbital MR images, potentially leading to measurement errors. Rescanning is time-consuming. Therefore, this study aims to develop an orbital MRI-based automated exophthalmos tool to minimize errors and ensure the stability and objectivity of the measurement results.

Participants
The retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital, and the requirement of individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived (2025PHB181-001).
  From December 2023 to January 2025, patients were deemed eligible for inclusion in the study if they satisfied the following criteria: 1) a diagnosis of TAO in accordance with Bartley’s diagnostic criteria (20); 2) completion of an orbital MR examination (including 2D and 3D T2-weighted MR sequences) at Peking University People’s Hospital; 3) no prior history of orbital surgery. Patients were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: 1) the MR image quality was insufficient for further analysis; 2) the interval between the orbital MR scan and Hertel measurement exceeded three days. 
Hertel-based Proptosis Measurement
Hertel-based proptosis measurements were performed by a single ophthalmologist. Subjects were instructed to maintain a forward gaze, and the small convexities on the Hertel exophthalmometer (66 Vision Tech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) were positioned on the temporal orbital rims of both eyes. When the two scales on the exophthalmometer aligned in the plane mirror, the scale value corresponding to the corneal tip image was recorded, measuring the proptosis.
MRI-based Proptosis Measurement 
Standardized orbital MRI examinations were performed using a 3T Siemens Prisma whole-body MRI system (Magnetom 3T Siemens, Prisma, Germany). Participants were instructed to lie in a supine position, and look forward, with their eyes closed naturally. 2D T2-weighted DIXON in-phase (IN) sequence (slice thickness = 2.5 mm, Repetition Time = 5700 ms, Echo Time = 84 ms, Flip Angle = 140°, Pixel Spacing = 0.56 mm × 0.56 mm) and 3D T2-weighted sequence (slice thickness = 0.6 mm, Repetition Time = 1400 ms, Echo Time = 120 ms, Flip Angle = 120°, Pixel Spacing = 0.56 mm × 0.56 mm) were used.
	Proptosis measurements using orbital MRI were conducted by three radiologists: one with 25 years of experience and two junior practitioners. The procedure involved identifying the maximum eyeball plane on the axial orbital image(Figure 2). A line connecting the lateral orbital rims, known as the interzygomatic line, was drawn(10). Subsequently, a line perpendicular to the interzygomatic line was extended to the anterior globe margin (specifically the corneal apex) to determine the proptosis. These measurements were conducted independently for each orbit. 
MRI-based Automated Assessment of Exophthalmos
The corneal apex-to-geometric center distance remains unchanged with rotation. According to the literature, exophthalmos changes due to ocular rotation are negligible(12,13). Therefore, as illustrated in Figure 2, exophthalmos can be considered equivalent to the vector sum of (AB ) ⃗ + (BC ) ⃗, or alternatively, the vector sum of (A'B' ) ⃗ + (B'C' ) ⃗. It's calculated based on two parameters: the corneal apex-to-geometric center distance(AB, A'B') and the vertical measurement from the geometric center to the anterior margin line of zygomatic process(BC, B'C'). Utilizing a deep learning-based algorithm for the development of an eyeball segmentation model enables the acquisition of the three-dimensional coordinates of both the corneal apex and the geometric center of the eyeball. Additionally, by implementing an automated identification model for the bilateral anterior zygomatic margins, the coordinates of the most anterior zygomatic projections can be determined. Through three-dimensional vector analysis, the corrected exophthalmometry value can subsequently be calculated.

Image Cropping and Segmentation
The 3D MRI sequences were anonymized and converted from DICOM to NIfTI format. Subsequently, image cropping was performed (specific identification and cropping methods, dimensions). Following cropping, the images were imported into 3D Slicer(Version 5.6.2, https://www.slicer.org.), where a single radiologist segmented the eyeballs and the anterior-most apex of the zygomatic process in 177 patients with TAO.
颧突最前缘自动识别Model construction

For automated identification of the anterior-most point of the zygomatic prominence, we developed a deep learning-based segmentation model using a U-Net architecture. The model was trained on 2D axial T2-weighted MRI slices extracted from the 3D orbital MRI sequences.

Data Preprocessing and Annotation: The 2D MRI slices (320×112 pixels) were extracted from the axial plane with maximum eyeball cross-sectional area. Manual annotations of zygomatic prominence locations were converted into circular binary masks with a radius of 5 pixels centered at each annotated point to account for potential inter-observer variability and provide more robust training targets. Image intensities were normalized using Z-score standardization (mean=0.5, standard deviation=0.5) to improve training stability.

Network Architecture: The U-Net model consisted of a symmetric encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections. The encoder pathway comprised four downsampling blocks, each containing two 3×3 convolutional layers followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation, with max pooling for downsampling. The decoder pathway used four upsampling blocks with transposed convolutions and concatenated skip connections to preserve spatial information. The initial feature map had 64 channels, doubling at each downsampling level up to 1024 channels at the bottleneck, then halving symmetrically in the decoder. The final output layer used a 1×1 convolution followed by sigmoid activation to generate probability maps for zygomatic prominence localization.

Training Protocol: The dataset was randomly split into training (80%) and validation (20%) sets. Training was performed for a maximum of 150 epochs using binary cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1×10⁻⁴. A learning rate scheduler reduced the learning rate by 50% when validation loss plateaued for 5 consecutive epochs. Early stopping was implemented with a patience of 10 epochs to prevent overfitting. Model performance was evaluated using precision, recall, and F1-score metrics on the validation set.

Eyeball auto-segmentation (hzy)
Based on 121 NIfTI format image data, we used UNet to achieve automatic segmentation of the eyeball. The U-Net network we utilized comprised four down/up sampling layers. The first convolutional layer had 16 kernels, which we doubled/halved when the feature map resolution was halved/doubled in the following layers. The output of the network had the same resolution as the input image. The dataset was divided into 65, 16, and 40 for training, validation, and testing, respectively. We carried out 200 epochs for training using an Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1e-4. The learning rate was gradually decayed to 1e-6 using a polynomial decay ('poly') strategy. The network was optimized using a combination of Dice loss and cross-entropy loss to improve segmentation performance and robustness.
Statistical Analysis
Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing RStudio (Version 2024.12.0+467). Mean values accompanied by standard deviations (SD) were reported. A paired t-test and Pearson correlation analysis were employed to evaluate differences and correlations between various methods or observers. The G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.6) determined that a sample size of 54 was necessary to achieve 95% statistical power. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Bland-Altman plot were utilized to assess method error. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
Results
Participants
According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 189 TAO patients were included (Figure 3). 12 orbital MR images were excluded due to poor image quality. Ultimately, the study comprised 2D and 3D MRI sequences (354 eyes), and simultaneous Hertel exophthalmometer measurements (144 eyes). The Automated Exophthalmos Tool was developed based on 3D MRI sequences, with 354 eyes being utilized to establish subsequent automated models for eyeball segmentation and zygomatic process recognition.

To validate the accuracy of the automatic segmentation, we invited radiologists to manually annotate the eyeball, which served as the ground truth for evaluation. The accuracy between the automatic segmentation and the manual reference was quantitatively assessed using the Dice similarity coefficient. On the testing set, the U-Net model achieved a high Dice coefficient of 0.979 ± 0.014, demonstrating excellent segmentation performance. This result indicates that the model is capable of accurately delineating the eyeball region with minimal discrepancy from the ground truth annotations. The low standard deviation further suggests strong consistency across different test samples. These findings highlight the model’s robustness and its potential for reliable application in automated ocular image analysis.

Model Performance Evaluation: To assess the automated zygomatic prominence identification model, we implemented a comprehensive evaluation protocol on the validation dataset. The model output probability maps were post-processed using connected component analysis to identify the zygomatic prominence locations.

Point Extraction Method: Given the bilateral nature of zygomatic prominences, each axial image was divided into upper and lower halves (at 160 pixels for 320×112 images). Within each half, regions with predicted probabilities exceeding 0.5 were identified using binary thresholding. Connected component analysis was applied to extract the largest connected region in each half, representing the most confident zygomatic prominence prediction. The centroid coordinates of each largest connected component were calculated as the final predicted zygomatic prominence locations.

Distance-based Accuracy Assessment: Model performance was quantitatively evaluated by measuring the Euclidean distance between predicted centroid coordinates and manually annotated ground truth points. For each validation sample, distances were calculated separately for upper and lower zygomatic prominences. The overall accuracy was assessed using mean distance metrics across all validation samples.

Discussion
This study investigated the impacts of different MRI sequences and observer variability on exophthalmos measurements in patients with thyroid-associated orbitopathy (TAO). No statistically significant differences were observed between measurements obtained by the same radiologists using 2D versus 3D MRI. Additionally, 2D and 3D MRI-based measurements showed no significant difference in correlation with Hertel-based exophthalmometry. In contrast, a previous CT-based study demonstrated that 3D CT exophthalmos measurements were more reliable than 2D CT in 50 TAO patients(14). This discrepancy may arise because 3D CT provides superior spatial resolution for identifying the corneal apex. However, even 2D T2-weighted MRI sequences—leveraging MRI’s high soft-tissue contrast—allow precise differentiation of the corneal apex from adjacent eyelid structures(15).	
   However, notable interobserver variability emerged in orbital MRI-based exophthalmos assessments. Potential contributing factors include: 1) Visual plane selection bias: Thin MRI slices necessitated subjective identification of the maximal cross-sectional eyeball plane, potentially leading to inconsistent selections among observers; 2) Experience-dependent anatomical landmarking: Variation in recognizing the anterior-most apex of the zygomatic process was influenced by observers' expertise levels. 
    In clinical practice, we identified suboptimal patient compliance during prolonged MRI scans, where deviations from required forward gaze (due to scan duration or strabismus) compromised measurement accuracy(14). Based on conventional measurement protocols and previous literature, we developed an automated eyeball segmentation model and an anterior zygomatic apex identification model. The geometric center of the segmented eyeball was computationally derived. In accordance with ocular anatomy, the anterior corneal apex—defined as the farthest point on the corneal surface from the geometric center—was automatically identified. Additionally, the maximal cross-sectional plane of the eyeball was determined by calculating the largest axial cross-sectional area across all slices. Within this plane, the coordinates of the anterior-most zygomatic apex were obtained using the automated identification model. 
    Through three-dimensional vector analysis, the corrected exophthalmometry value was calculated as the vector sum of two components: the distance from the anterior corneal apex to the geometric center (directional protrusion vector) and the perpendicular projection from the geometric center to the bilateral zygomatic reference line (compensating for positional deviations).

These findings confirm that 3D vector-corrected exophthalmos measurements are both accurate and clinically feasible. The automated exophthalmometry tool not only enhances measurement objectivity and precision but also improves operational efficiency, facilitating timely diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring in orbital disorders.
    Furthermore, a strong correlation between orbital MRI-based and Hertel exophthalmometer measurements was demonstrated in 122 TAO patients, though inherent interobserver variability persisted. This discrepancy is likely attributable to variations in measurement methodologies and differences in patient positioning during assessments. Therefore, in clinical practice, direct comparisons between the two methods should be avoided when evaluating changes of exophthalmometry.
   However, this study is currently limited as a single-center investigation and requires validation across multiple centers with larger sample sizes. Through code optimization, interactive collaboration between radiologists and model outputs can be enabled, such as allowing manual adjustment and confirmation of the anterior zygomatic apex, thereby continuously enhancing model performance while improving measurement efficiency and accuracy.
Conclusion
Orbital MRI-based exophthalmos measurements showed no significant differences between 2D and 3D MRI sequences but exhibited statistically significant interobserver variability among radiologists. The Orbital MRI-based Deep Learning Automated Exophthalmos Tool enables rapid and objective quantification of exophthalmometry, providing reproducible and accurate data to inform clinical decision-making for patients with TAO.
